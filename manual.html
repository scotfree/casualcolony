§1 Overview

What is this?

Casual Colony is a tap-driven puzzle about running a power grid that has a small colony living inside it. There is no clock — every tap is a turn, and turns are the only kind of time the game keeps. You wire tiles together, and every turn energy flows out from your generators and pays to keep them running. Keep a population fed on what your farms grow, and mines turn a fed colony back into energy. You win by having enough of the board running at once; you lose when your reserve empties with tiles you can't power.

There is exactly one move: feed a tile. Your reserve then carries that tile's cost every turn, and a cascade starts there and spreads as far as the wiring and the generation allow. You can't switch anything off again, so a tap is a commitment rather than a toggle — and the whole game is whether your reserve can keep carrying what you've committed to while the cascades do the actual work.

§2 Quick Start

Two taps that teach the whole model

Open any level with a power plant in it.

  1. Tap the power plant. Your reserve now pays its cost of 1, every turn, for as long as it runs. In exchange its whole generation of 5 goes into the grid it's wired to — and the cascade spreads outward from it, paying 1 for each crystal it reaches until the 5 is gone. One tap, six tiles lit.
  2. Tap a crystal the cascade didn't reach. Now your reserve is paying 2 a turn instead of 1, and that crystal becomes a second place a cascade starts. Watch the ⚡ counter: it drops by 2 every turn now, and it will keep dropping.

The crystals cost the grid, not you. Your reserve only ever pays for tiles you tapped — so a plant carrying five crystals costs you 1 a turn, and a plant carrying nothing also costs you 1 a turn. Reach is free; commitments aren't.
The number that matters

A power plant makes 5. Every crystal past it costs 1. So one tap on a plant is worth six tiles if they're wired to it, and one tile if they aren't. Everything you do in this game is trying to make that first number happen instead of the second.

There is no undo

You can't switch a tile back off. Once you've fed something, your reserve carries it until the reserve runs out — and when it can't carry everything you've fed, none of it runs and the run is over. So the pool ticking down is the clock, and every tap makes it tick faster. Feed plants; let cascades do the rest.

§3 Mechanics

How everything works

One move, two budgets

Tapping a tile feeds it. That's the only move there is — you can't switch anything off, and there's nothing else to click. Feeding a tile does two things: your reserve takes on its cost every turn from now on, and a cascade starts there.

The two budgets never mix, and holding them apart is most of understanding the game:

  • Your reserve (the ⚡ counter) pays for the tiles you fed, every turn, and nothing else.
  • A grid's generation pays for the cascade, and nothing else.

So a big reserve can't push a cascade one tile further, and a plant sitting on unused generation can't pay your bills. Costs are upkeep — crystals 1, residential and farm 2, a mine 3, a drain 4, every turn it runs, never a one-off price.

How energy travels

Energy starts at your generators and flows outward through connected tiles, paying each one's cost as it reaches it. Every unit is spent exactly once, so a wide network genuinely costs more than a narrow one — splitting a run in two doesn't get you two runs' worth of energy.

A fed power plant hands its grid the whole 5 — you paid its cost of 1 out of your reserve, so there's nothing to net off. Five crystals, or two farms and a crystal, or a mine and two crystals. That's the number to keep in your head.

Move that drain to the far end of the run instead and the same tiles all fit — four crystals and then the drain, with the drain the only thing that goes without. Nothing changed but the order the energy meets them in.

Two consequences worth building around:

  • Placement matters, not just totals. An expensive tile near a generator can starve a whole cheap run behind it. Put your loads at the far end and the same tiles all fit.
  • Ties are never broken. If several equally-priced tiles are all reached at once and there isn't enough for all of them, none of them light. The game will not pick a winner by position, ever — so a symmetric layout stays symmetric.

Wiring, and what counts as one grid

Tiles are connected if they're orthogonally adjacent, both switched on, and both carry the direction between them. Plain crystal, plants and colony buildings connect all four ways. Red crystal connects only north/south and green only east/west.

What that buys you is parallel runs with no gap between them: a 2×2 block of red crystal is two independent columns sitting flush, where plain crystal would merge into one blob. It does not let two runs cross — a green tile lying across a red run breaks it, because that tile only carries one axis.

Each connected grid spends its own generation. A plant's spare energy cannot reach a grid it isn't wired to, however close it looks on screen.

Your reserve is a clock

The ⚡ number in the HUD is your reserve, and your upkeep is the total cost of every tile you've fed. Each turn the upkeep comes out and any working mines pay back in. If upkeep is bigger than what the mines bring, the counter ticks down — and you can read exactly how many turns you have left off the screen.

When the reserve can't cover the whole bill, nothing you fed runs — not some of it, all of it goes dark at once, and that's the loss. It's abrupt on purpose: the alternative is the game deciding which of your tiles matters. The warning isn't that moment, it's the several turns of countdown before it.

Feed generators, not tiles

Feeding a plant costs you 1 a turn and can light six tiles. Feeding a lone crystal costs you 1 a turn and lights one. Same bill, wildly different value — which is why the game is about wiring things to a generator rather than paying for them one at a time.

Drain — the tile that just costs

A drain costs 4 a turn and gives nothing back — exactly a power plant's entire surplus. It's still wire, so energy passes through it, which makes it a very effective plug in the middle of a run. Switching it off is free, and often the whole answer.

Mines only pay out while the colony is fed and has at least one resident — a colony of zero is trivially "fed" against any food capacity, but there's nobody there to work a mine. A starving colony's mines sit idle too, and the shortfall bleeds energy on top.

A mine is the only thing that pays into your reserve rather than back into its own grid. That's deliberate: it means a mine never helps power the grid it's standing in — that grid has to carry its cost of 3 out of its own generation — and it's why mines are the only way to refill what you spend.

There is no undo

Nothing can be switched back off. A tap is a commitment for the rest of the run, so the question before every tap is "can I carry this?" rather than "can I afford this?" — and a tile the cascade already reaches is never worth feeding, because it's running for free.

Fog — what you can see, and therefore do

Some levels are fogged. On those, you can only see a short distance around anything that's running — and you can only tap what you can see. A tile in the dark isn't an expensive move, it's not a move at all; tapping it does nothing. Getting something running extends your sight from there, so the board unfolds as you work outward.

Sight behaves nothing like the flow of energy, on purpose. Energy travels only through connected tiles and pays each one's cost; fog is a plain distance that ignores terrain entirely and crosses desert freely. So fog often shows you a cluster your grid can't reach — which is exactly the hint that it's worth wiring toward, or worth spending reserve on directly. Once you've seen a tile it stays on screen, dimmed, even if it falls dark again: fog never makes you re-learn a board you've already explored, it just stops you acting at a distance.

Two things fog can never do. It can't hide anything that's running — a running tile always lights its own square — so it can never strip progress from view, and it can never hide a tile that's paying for itself.

When you're not sure what just happened

Tap log in the top right. It shows what your most recent tap did — what your grid made and drew, which tiles came on or went dark, what happened to population and food, where the energy went — and next to each effect, the short reason it followed. It's the fastest way to see why a mine earned nothing, or why the energy stopped where it did, without working backwards from the board. It only ever shows the last turn, so check it while the surprise is fresh.

How a run ends

Both endings are checked after every turn:

  • You win as soon as the level's goal is met. Most levels ask for a fraction of the whole board to be running at once — a snapshot, not a total, so you have to hold it, not just have touched it. Some ask instead for a fraction of the board to have been revealed, which is monotonic and can't be lost.
  • You lose to a blackout — your reserve can no longer pay the upkeep on everything you've fed, so none of it runs. Note that a cascade going only part way is not a loss: tiles the generation didn't reach never cost you anything and were never yours. Only the tiles you tapped can bankrupt you.

A power plant nets +4, and a drain costs exactly that — one drain cancels one plant. A plant keeps itself running because it banks enough to cover its own cost across the turn boundary; energy can't pay for the turn that produces it.
A mine pays into your reserve, never into its own grid — so that grid must cover its 3 out of its own generation, and a mine standing alone with a plant costs the pair 4 of the plant's 5. It also needs the colony fed with at least one resident somewhere on the board; not a worker each.
Food isn't spent, it's a ceiling: you're fed while people ≤ food. Cross the line and every mine stops paying while you bleed 1 energy per person over it, every turn — and since nothing can be switched off, the only cure is getting another farm running.
§4 Making Your Own

Editing levels and tiles, and keeping what you make

Everything so far has been about playing. The game also lets you change the board, redraw what a building looks like, and — this is the part that matters — get those changes out of the browser and into the project itself, so they survive and everyone else sees them.

Edit mode

Tap edit in the top right. Tapping a tile now opens a picker instead of playing a turn: choose any building type and that cell becomes it. The two right-hand buttons change jobs while you're editing — save as saves your board under a name you type, and restart saves over the level as loaded and drops you back into playing it. Editing only ever changes tile types; the level's numbers (energy budget, completion goal, the colony knobs in Fig. 4) are set when a level is authored and aren't part of what edit mode touches.

The picker also has a starts visible checkbox. On a fogged level that's how you set where the run begins: with nothing visible there'd be nothing tappable at all, so a fogged board needs at least one seed tile. Ticking it doesn't change what the tile is — it's a separate property of that square, so the checkbox stays open while you also pick a type.

Drawing a tile's icon

Every row in the tile picker has a pencil button. It opens a 10×10 pixel grid, pre-filled with that building's current icon, where tapping a pixel flips it on or off. Saving replaces that icon everywhere — a custom mine is a custom mine on every level, because an icon belongs to the building type rather than to one cell or one board.

Where your edits actually live

This game is static files with no server behind it, so an edit is saved to your browser's local storage. That means it exists on that device only — not on your laptop, not for anyone else — and clearing your browser's site data deletes it. The export flow below is how an edit stops being temporary.

Getting edits into the project

Open the level button, then levels & icons… at the bottom of the list. You get four things:

Each export is a whole file, not a fragment — so you replace the file wholesale rather than merging anything by hand, and the change shows up as a clean diff.
  1. Export. Pick export levels or export icons. The text appears in a box with copy, and on a phone a share button too — which is the quick way to AirDrop it to a laptop or send it to yourself. If neither works, the text is right there to select by hand.
  2. Replace the file and commit. Paste it over levels/levels.json or icons-data.js in the repo. Once that's pushed, your level or icon ships with the game for everyone.
  3. Clear local edits. Come back and tap it (twice — it asks for confirmation). Until you do, your local copies keep shadowing the identical shipped ones, which quietly means later changes to the project file get ignored on your device.

Import is the same trip backwards: one paste box that works out on its own whether you handed it levels or icons. It's how you move work between a phone and a laptop, and how you get everything back if a browser ever wipes its storage. Bad input is rejected with a reason rather than half-loaded — a level that wouldn't actually play never makes it in.

§5 Open Questions

What's undecided, and what's not built yet

Casual Colony is a small game that grows one system at a time. Some of what's below is genuinely undecided; the rest is scoped out on purpose, for now.

Still open

Should winning ever be spatial, not just a percentage?
Right now the win condition only cares about the fraction of the whole board activated — not where those tiles are. Contiguous territory, or holding a shape, is a different kind of puzzle that hasn't been tried.
Should placing buildings ever cost energy mid-run?
Right now you can only change what a tile is in the separate level editor, outside a real run, for free. Spending energy to place a building as part of a run — instead of just activating what's already there — is a different game and hasn't been built.
Where should more job types plug in?
Mining was the first colony "job." Sensor towers (revealing fog of war) and defense against dangers were both part of the original pitch — the colony economy was built generally enough for a new job to opt in the same way mining did, as one flag on a building, but neither exists yet.
